# Breathing and circulation: how oxygen reaches your cells

## Why your body needs both systems

Every cell in your body releases energy from food through cellular respiration. This process uses oxygen and produces carbon dioxide as a waste product. The respiratory system brings oxygen into the body and removes carbon dioxide. The circulatory system carries both gases between the lungs and the rest of the body. Neither system can do the job alone.

## The path air takes

When you breathe in, air enters through the nose or mouth. Hairs and mucus in the nasal cavity trap dust, and the air is warmed and moistened. The air passes the pharynx (throat) and the larynx (voice box), then travels down the trachea (windpipe). Rings of cartilage keep the trachea open.

The trachea divides into two bronchi, one for each lung. Inside the lungs the bronchi branch into smaller and smaller tubes called bronchioles, which end in clusters of tiny air sacs called alveoli.

## Gas exchange in the alveoli

The lungs contain hundreds of millions of alveoli. Their walls are only one cell thick and each one is wrapped in a net of capillaries. Oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveoli, where its concentration is high, into the blood, where its concentration is lower. Carbon dioxide diffuses the other way, from the blood into the alveoli, and is breathed out.

Three features make this exchange fast: the very large total surface area of the alveoli, their thin walls, and their moist lining, which lets gases dissolve before they diffuse.

## How breathing works

Breathing is driven mainly by the diaphragm, a dome-shaped muscle under the lungs. When you inhale, the diaphragm contracts and flattens, and the muscles between the ribs pull the rib cage up and out. The chest cavity gets bigger, the air pressure inside the lungs drops below the pressure outside, and air flows in.

When you exhale, the diaphragm relaxes and moves back up, the rib cage moves down and in, the chest cavity gets smaller, and air is pushed out.

## The heart and its four chambers

The heart is a muscular pump about the size of your fist. It has four chambers: two upper atria and two lower ventricles. The right side of the heart receives oxygen-poor blood from the body and pumps it to the lungs. The left side receives oxygen-rich blood from the lungs and pumps it to the rest of the body.

Valves between the chambers and at the exits of the ventricles keep blood flowing in one direction. The left ventricle has the thickest muscular wall because it must pump blood all the way around the body.

## Blood vessels

Arteries carry blood away from the heart. Their walls are thick and elastic so they can handle high pressure. Veins carry blood back to the heart. They have thinner walls and contain valves that stop blood from flowing backward. Capillaries are the smallest vessels. Their walls are one cell thick, which lets oxygen, nutrients, carbon dioxide and other wastes pass between the blood and the body's cells.

## Two circuits working together

Blood travels in two circuits. In pulmonary circulation, blood goes from the right ventricle to the lungs, picks up oxygen, and returns to the left atrium. In systemic circulation, blood goes from the left ventricle to the tissues of the body, delivers oxygen, and returns to the right atrium.

When you exercise, your muscles need more oxygen and produce more carbon dioxide. You breathe faster and more deeply, and your heart beats faster and pumps more blood with each beat. This is how the two systems respond together to the body's needs.

## Keeping both systems healthy

Smoking damages the lining of the airways and the walls of the alveoli, and it makes the heart work harder. Regular physical activity strengthens the heart muscle and helps the lungs work efficiently. Avoiding second-hand smoke and wearing a well-fitted mask during heavy smoke or haze protect the airways. A balanced diet that limits excess salt and saturated fat helps keep blood vessels healthy.
