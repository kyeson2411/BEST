# Making inferences while reading

## What an inference is

An inference is a logical conclusion you reach by combining clues in a text with what you already know. Writers do not state everything directly. They show actions, details and dialogue, and they expect readers to work out the rest.

## Clues plus knowledge

A useful formula is: text clues + what I know = inference. Text clues can be a character's actions, words or feelings, details of the setting, or the writer's choice of words. What you know comes from your own experience and from earlier reading.

## Steps for making an inference

1. Ask a question that the text does not answer directly, such as "Why did the character leave early?"
2. Find two or three clues in the text that relate to the question.
3. Connect the clues to what you already know.
4. State your inference and point to the evidence that supports it.
5. Check whether the rest of the text supports or challenges your inference.

## A short practice passage

Mara stood at the jeepney stop for the third time that week, clutching her umbrella even though the sky was clear. When the jeep finally came, she let two older women board first, then squeezed into the last seat near the door. She kept glancing at her wristwatch and tapping her foot.

From the clues "for the third time that week", the repeated glances at her watch and the tapping foot, together with what we know about waiting for public transport, we can infer that Mara is worried about being late. Letting the older women board first suggests that she is considerate even when she is in a hurry.

## Common mistakes

- Making a guess that has no support in the text.
- Ignoring clues that contradict your idea.
- Confusing an inference with a summary of what the text says directly.

## Inference or prediction?

A prediction is a kind of inference about what will happen next. Like any inference, a prediction should be supported by clues from the text.
